1879

Beginning of Scientific Psychology
1879

Wilhelm Wundt

  • Established a psychology laboratory in Leipzig, Germany.
  • Considered a major founder of psychology as a scientific discipline.
  • Studied consciousness using objective introspection.
  • Focused on basic components: thoughts, emotions and sensations.
Key point Wundt → 1879 → Leipzig → Objective Introspection
In depth — press D or click
  • Trained as a physician; worked as an assistant to the physicist-physiologist Hermann von Helmholtz.
  • He was the first person to call himself a psychologist, and is widely called the “father of experimental psychology”.
  • Objective introspection meant trained self-observation under strict experimental control — not casual armchair reflection.
  • His lab used instruments such as chronoscopes to time mental reactions, studying sensation, attention and reaction time.
From Laboratory to Movement
1883

Leipzig Becomes a Centre

  • 1874: his Principles of Physiological Psychology — the first textbook of experimental psychology.
  • 1883: the first psychology journal, Philosophische Studien, and official university recognition of the laboratory.
  • Students arrived from all over the world — about 185 doctoral dissertations, 70 by foreign students.
Key point Wundt's students → carried psychology across the world
In depth — press D or click
  • The journal ran as Philosophische Studien (1883–1903), then as Psychologische Studien (1905–1917).
  • Eighteen of his foreign doctoral students were American, so he sits in the “family tree” of most early American psychologists.
  • In a 1991 survey, historians of psychology ranked Wundt first for all-time eminence, with William James and Freud second and third.

Early Schools of Thought

Structuralism & Functionalism

Structuralism

Edward Titchener

  • Student of Wundt; took his ideas to America.
  • Studied the structure of the mind.
  • Basic sensations and emotions.
Functionalism

William James

  • Principles of Psychology, 1890.
  • The purpose and function of consciousness.
  • How the mind helps us adapt and survive.
Easy memory Titchener = Structure | James = Function
In depth — press D or click
  • Titchener likened the mind to chemistry: just as water breaks into hydrogen and oxygen, experience breaks into basic sensations. His method was strict introspection — describe the raw sensation, not the object's name.
  • At Cornell he built the largest doctoral programme of his time; his first student, Margaret Floy Washburn, became the first woman to earn a psychology PhD (1894). He also introduced the English word “empathy” (1909).
  • James trained as a physician but never practised; Principles took him twelve years and ran to about 1,200 pages. It gave us the “stream of consciousness”.
  • The James–Lange theory: we don't run because we fear the bear — we fear it because we notice ourselves running.
Gestalt Psychology

Max Wertheimer & the Gestalt Psychologists

“The whole is greater than the sum of its parts.”
  • Focused on sensation and perception.
  • People naturally organise information into whole patterns.
  • An experience can't always be understood from its parts.
  • Later influenced cognitive psychology.
ExampleA melody is more than individual notes — the relationship between the notes creates the melody.
In depth — press D or click
  • “Gestalt” is German for form or shape. Key figures: Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Köhler.
  • It began with Wertheimer's phi phenomenon (1912): two lights flashing in turn look like one moving light, though nothing moves.
  • A reaction against structuralism's habit of splitting experience into elements.
  • Classic principles: proximity, similarity, closure, continuity and figure–ground. Designers still use them today.
Psychoanalysis

Sigmund Freud

  • Focused on the unconscious mind.
  • Hidden urges and desires could be repressed.
  • Unconscious conflicts could influence behaviour.
  • Stressed early childhood experiences.
  • Strongly influenced psychotherapy.
Remember Freud → Unconscious mind + Childhood
In depth — press D or click
  • A Vienna neurologist whose tools were free association and dream analysis; The Interpretation of Dreams appeared at the end of 1899 (dated 1900).
  • His model of personality has three parts: the id (urges), the ego (reality) and the superego (conscience).
  • Defence mechanisms such as repression and denial protect us from anxiety.
  • Often criticised as hard to test scientifically — Wundt himself stayed sceptical of the unconscious — yet the “talking cure” shaped modern therapy.
Behaviourism

John B. Watson

  • Developed Behaviourism: observable, measurable behaviour.
  • Behaviour is learned through conditioning.
Little Albert

Watson and Rosalie Rayner paired a white rat with a loud noise. Albert came to fear the rat — fear could be learned.

Mary Cover Jones

Showed that learned fears can be reduced through counterconditioning.

Remember Little Albert → Fear learned | Little Peter → Fear reduced
In depth — press D or click
  • Watson's 1913 article “Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It” is often called the behaviourist manifesto; he rejected introspection and built on Pavlov's conditioning.
  • Little Albert (about 1920) was a baby of roughly nine months; his fear spread to similar furry things. The study would not pass today's ethics rules — and helped push research ethics forward.
  • Mary Cover Jones (1924) treated a young boy, Peter, by showing a rabbit at a distance while he ate, then moving it gradually closer. She is often called the “mother of behaviour therapy”.
The Modern Era

Modern Names in Psychology

Humanistic

Abraham Maslow

Hierarchy of needs: people are driven to grow, from basic needs up to self-actualisation.

Humanistic

Carl Rogers

Person-centred therapy: empathy and unconditional positive regard help people change.

Behavioural

B. F. Skinner

Operant conditioning: behaviour is shaped by its consequences, reward and punishment.

Social learning

Albert Bandura

We learn by watching others, and our belief in ourselves, self-efficacy, shapes what we try.

Child development

Jean Piaget

Children think in distinct stages, from sensorimotor to formal operations.

Judgement

Daniel Kahneman

With Amos Tversky, showed that mental shortcuts cause predictable errors. Nobel Prize in Economics, 2002.

Remember Maslow and Rogers = growth | Skinner and Bandura = learning | Piaget = child thinking | Kahneman = thinking errors
In depth — more modern names
  • George Miller (1956) showed we hold about seven items in short-term memory, and Ulric Neisser's 1967 book Cognitive Psychology gave the field its name.
  • Elizabeth Loftus showed that eyewitness memory can be changed by how a question is asked.
  • John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth built attachment theory: early bonds shape later relationships.
  • Martin Seligman led the positive psychology movement from 1998, studying strengths and wellbeing, not only illness.
  • Applied psychology: Wundt's students Münsterberg and Scott opened the way for industrial and organisational psychology, followed by the Hawthorne studies of the 1920s and early 1930s.

Part Two · The Endocrine System

The Body's Chemical Messengers

  • Glands release hormones straight into the bloodstream.
  • Each hormone acts on target cells that carry the matching receptors.
  • Main glands: hypothalamus, pituitary, pineal, thyroid, parathyroids, adrenals, pancreas, ovaries and testes.
  • Works with the nervous system to control mood, stress, sleep, growth, metabolism and reproduction.
Nervous systemEndocrine system
MessengerNeurotransmitters crossing a synapseHormones travelling in the blood
SpeedMillisecondsSeconds to hours, sometimes longer
ReachSpecific, targeted cellsWidespread: any cell with the receptor
DurationBriefLonger-lasting
Remember Nervous system = fast and precise | Endocrine system = slower, widespread, long-lasting
In depth — press D or click
  • The hypothalamus links the two systems: it is brain tissue that controls the pituitary gland.
  • Hormone levels are kept in balance by negative feedback, like a thermostat switching a heater off once the room is warm.
  • Some chemicals, such as dopamine and noradrenaline, work in both systems. Later slides compare their two jobs.
Part Two · The Pituitary Gland

The “Master Gland”

  • A pea-sized gland at the base of the brain, hanging from the hypothalamus by a stalk.
  • Two parts: the anterior (front) lobe and the posterior (back) lobe.
  • Anterior: the hypothalamus sends releasing and inhibiting hormones down tiny blood vessels, and the lobe makes its own hormones.
  • Posterior: oxytocin and ADH are made in the hypothalamus and released here along nerve fibres.
  • Called “master” because its hormones control other glands, yet the hypothalamus controls the pituitary.
Chain of command Hypothalamus → Pituitary → Target glands
In depth — press D or click
  • The posterior lobe grows out of the hypothalamus itself, so it is neural tissue. The anterior lobe develops from the lining of the embryo's mouth region.
  • The hypothalamus controls most anterior hormones by releasing signals. Prolactin is the exception: it is mainly held back by an inhibiting signal, dopamine.
  • Damage or tumours here can cause hormone problems that show up as changes in mood, energy, sex drive and growth.

Part Two · Stress and the Mind

The Stress Axis: Hypothalamus, Pituitary, Adrenals

  • Stressor noticed by the brain
  • Hypothalamus releases CRH
  • Anterior pituitary releases ACTH
  • Adrenal cortex releases cortisol
  • Cortisol frees up energy and helps the body cope with short-term stress.
  • It then tells the hypothalamus and pituitary to slow down: a negative feedback loop.
  • A faster route: sympathetic nerves tell the adrenal medulla to release adrenaline and noradrenaline for fight or flight.
  • In depression, studies often find raised cortisol and CRH, weaker feedback and a smaller hippocampus.
Careful These are links seen in studies, not proof that cortisol alone causes depression
In depth — press D or click
  • Cortisol follows a daily rhythm, rising in the early morning and falling through the day.
  • Vasopressin (ADH) boosts CRH's push on the pituitary during stress.
  • Too much cortisol (Cushing's syndrome) commonly brings depression or anxiety; too little (Addison's disease) commonly brings fatigue and low mood.
Part Two · Hormones and Mental Health

When Hormones Meet Mood

Thyroid hormones

Too little (hypothyroidism) is linked to depression; too much (hyperthyroidism) is linked more to anxiety. Treating the thyroid problem often helps mood.

Stress hormones

Chronic stress can keep the stress axis switched on. Over-active stress hormones are linked to depression and anxiety.

Sex hormones

Oestrogen, progesterone and testosterone influence mood, energy and drive. Big hormonal changes, such as after childbirth, are periods of higher risk for mood problems.

Melatonin

The pineal gland releases it in the dark, setting the sleep–wake rhythm. Disturbed body clocks are linked to sleep problems and to depression with a seasonal pattern.

Hormones influence mood, but mental health problems have many causes: biology, thoughts, experiences and surroundings.

In depth — press D or click
  • The link runs both ways: stress and mood change hormone release, and hormones change mood.
  • Many of these are associations. In thyroid disorders, researchers are still unsure how far the link is causal.
  • A hormone change is usually a risk factor, rarely the only cause, so treatment looks at the whole person.

Remember Same molecule | different place | different job
In depth — press D or click
  • Dopamine in the blood cannot cross the blood–brain barrier, so it does not act as a brain chemical. Parkinson's is treated with L-DOPA, which can enter the brain and be turned into dopamine.
  • The brain makes its own serotonin, in the raphe nuclei of the brainstem. Gut serotonin is a separate pool.
Part Two · Fact-Check

Dopamine: Myths and Facts

Myth

Too much dopamine causes Parkinson's disease

Fact

Parkinson's comes from the loss of dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra, so movement circuits get too little dopamine. Symptoms usually appear after 60 to 80 percent or more of these cells are lost. Treatment aims to raise dopamine, for example with L-DOPA.

Myth

The dopamine hormone is the happiness hormone

Fact

As a hormone, dopamine's best-known job is to stop the pituitary releasing prolactin. Reward and motivation come from dopamine acting as a neurotransmitter in brain pathways, and research suggests it drives wanting and motivation more than pleasure itself.

Too much

Dopamine and psychosis

Over-active dopamine in the brain's mesolimbic pathway is linked to psychosis, in the dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia, though it is only part of the story. Dopamine-raising drugs such as L-DOPA can cause psychotic side effects, and antipsychotics work by blocking D2 receptors.

Remember Too little in movement circuits = Parkinson's | Too much in reward circuits = psychosis risk | As a hormone = controls prolactin
In depth — the four dopamine pathways
  • Nigrostriatal: movement. Loss of its neurons causes Parkinson's.
  • Mesolimbic: reward and motivation. Over-activity is linked to psychosis.
  • Mesocortical: thinking and motivation.
  • Tuberoinfundibular: hypothalamus to pituitary, controlling prolactin. Blocking it can raise prolactin.
Part Two · Key Takeaways

What to Remember

  • Hormones are chemical messengers in the blood; the hypothalamus and pituitary are the control centre.
  • The anterior pituitary releases ACTH, TSH, GH, prolactin, FSH and LH; the posterior releases oxytocin and ADH.
  • The stress axis links stress to mood; chronic stress is linked to depression and anxiety.
  • The same chemical can do different jobs as a neurotransmitter and as a hormone.
  • Parkinson's is too little dopamine in movement circuits, not too much.
